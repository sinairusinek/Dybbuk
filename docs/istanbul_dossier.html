5 · What the lexicon narrates: three Istanbul waves

Reading the 79 entries together, Istanbul appears in the Leksikon in three distinct historical layers:

The Goldfaden-era Orient (1870s–1880s)

Cezar Grinberg locates himself in Constantinople in 1875 and stages a biblical operetta on the Joseph story — „מכירת יוסף" with up to 100 chorus and ballet — then tours it through Turkey and Romania to Iași. Chaim Abramovitsh and Itsik-Mendl Bergman performed in it; Israel Gradner and Goldfaden himself passed through in 1878–79 before returning to the Odessa troupe. Velvel Zbarzher's Broder-singer feud and death (1883) close this layer. Professor Moyshe Hurwitz fled to Constantinople around 1886 en route to London — and reappears, hungry, playing Galata with amateurs in Kornfeld's childhood memory.

The touring circuit (1890s–1914)

Istanbul is a standard stop between Romania, Bulgaria, Egypt and the Caucasus: the Gelis troupe (a full year), Kalman Jawiler's 17-year circuit (Galicia–Bukovina–Romania–Constantinople–Alexandria–Cairo), Krauze's troupes (with a contract from a certain Kornblit of Constantinople; Pinski's פֿאַמיליע צבי staged there), Leopold Kanner's troupe (1912), Anshel Schorr's 1888–89 visit where the operetta „גבור החייל" drew Ottoman notables. Leon Kornfeld — the entry with the most mentions (9) — sent the Leksikon his own history of Yiddish theater in Constantinople and ran the Concordia; Roze Friedman's café-singer career there is documented through his testimony.

The refugee stage (1918–1923)

After the pogroms, war and revolution, Constantinople becomes an emigration funnel: Clara Young's troupe plays there in 1918 and 1920 (Jenny Zeltser, Morris Braun), Sholem Brin founds a Yiddish folk theater with Dr. Vladimir Tiomkin and Joint members, Peter Amatin plays with a group of refugee actors from Russia (1919), Feli Bakman spends half of 1923 there before Canada, Alex Amasia plays seven months before America. Gabriel Zar runs Yiddish theater there for 15 months until 1921. The city then largely exits the narrative.

Two non-theatrical threads also surface: M.-L. Lilienblum's play agitating against the traffickers selling Jewish daughters צו שאַנדע אין קאָנסטאַנטינאָפּאָל, and Avrom Frumkin studying Turkish and jurisprudence there (1891) at the start of his journalistic career.
